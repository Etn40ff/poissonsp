\section{Introduction}

Shortly after cluster algebras were first introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky in~\cite{FZ01},
Gekhtman, Shapiro and Vainshtein initiated a systematic
study of the interaction between the combinatorics of cluster mutations and
the geometry of Poisson brackets~\cite{GSV03}.
The central notion introduced there is that of a
Poisson bracket \emph{compatible} with a cluster structure: a
bracket $\{\cdot,\cdot\}$ on the ambient field of rational functions
is compatible with a seed $(\mathbf{x},B)$ if the cluster variables
$x_1,\dots,x_N$ in $\mathbf{x}$ are \emph{log-canonical},
\[
  \{x_i,x_j\} = \omega_{ij}\, x_i x_j, \qquad \omega_{ij}\in \mathbb{Z} \text{ or } \mathbb{Q},
\]
and the resulting coefficient matrix $\Omega = (c_{ij})$ satisfies
an explicit linear compatibility relation with the exchange matrix
$B$ of the seed. When such a compatible pair exists, mutations
become, up to rescaling, Poisson automorphisms between coordinate
charts, cluster monomials give privileged log-canonical
coordinates, and the symplectic foliation of the Poisson variety is
governed by the combinatorics of frozen and Casimir-type cluster
variables. This program was carried out in depth in the monograph
\cite{GSV10}, which remains the standard reference for the subject
and treats, among other examples, Grassmannians, double Bruhat
cells, and directed networks from this unified perspective.

The prototypical and best understood family of examples is furnished
by simple complex Lie groups $\mathcal{G}$ equipped with the
\emph{standard} (Sklyanin) Poisson--Lie structure. Restricted to a
double Bruhat cell $\mathcal{G}^{u,v}$, this bracket is compatible,
in the sense above, with the cluster structure on
$\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{G}^{u,v})$ constructed by Berenstein, Fomin and
Zelevinsky out of generalized minors associated to a reduced word
for the pair $(u,v)$ \cite{BFZ05}; the initial seed is log-canonical,
and the compatibility persists under mutation to every other seed.
This result is established uniformly for every simple $\mathcal{G}$ in~\cite{GSV12cluster}.


The standard Poisson--Lie bracket is only one member of a much larger family of Poisson structures arising from the classification of Belavin and Drinfeld. 
In ~\cite{BD82}, Belavin and Drinfeld classified the solutions of the classical Yang--Baxter equation
on a simple Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$. 
Their classification is given in terms of discrete combinatorial data consisting of two subsets $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2$ of the set of simple roots of $\mathfrak{g}$ together with an isometry $\gamma:\Gamma_1\to\Gamma_2$. 
Such a triple is referred to as Belavin--Drinfeld (BD) data.

The trivial BD data, namely $\Gamma_1=\Gamma_2=\emptyset$, correspond to the standard Poisson--Lie bracket, which is compatible with the standard cluster structure introduced in~\cite{BFZ05}. 
More generally, nontrivial BD data give rise to Poisson brackets that are expected to be compatible with cluster structures different from the standard one. 
These cluster structures are often referred to as \emph{non-standard} or \emph{exotic}, following the terminology introduced in~\cite{GSV17exotic}.

Motivated by this perspective, Gekhtman, Shapiro, and Vainshtein 
conjectured in~\cite{GSV12cluster} 
that every nontrivial Belavin--Drinfeld datum admits a compatible cluster structure on 
$\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{G})$, generally distinct from the standard cluster structure. 
Their conjecture establishes a correspondence between Belavin--Drinfeld data and cluster structures on $\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{G})$ and specifies several expected properties of both the correspondence and the resulting cluster structures.

The conjecture has been verified in a number of cases.
In~\cite{GSV12cluster} it was proved or the trivial BD data for every simple Lie group $\mathcal{G}$
and for any BD data for $SL_n$ where $n<5$. Shortly after it was found to be true for $SL_5$~\cite{Es14exotic}
and for minimal size BD data, where $|\Gamma_1|=|\Gamma_2|=1$ on $SL_n$~\cite{Es17exotic01,Es17exotic02}.
Later on in~\cite{GSV24plethora} it was established for all aperiodic BD data in the case $\mathcal{G}=GL_n$. 
To the best of our knowledge, however, at the time of writing no examples beyond the $GL_n$ and $SL_n$ cases are known.

\section{Framework and Definitions}


\subsection{Cluster algebras of geometric type}

We recall the basic definitions, following Fomin--Zelevinsky
\cite{FZ01} and Berenstein--Fomin--Zelevinsky \cite{BFZ05}; a
detailed exposition close to the one used here can also be found in
\cite{GSV12cluster}.

Let $\mathcal{P}$ be a free multiplicative abelian group of finite
rank $m$, with generators $g_1,\dots,g_m$, called the
\emph{coefficient group}, and let $\mathcal{F}$ be the field of
rational functions in $n$ independent variables over the field of
fractions of $\mathbb{Z}\mathcal{P}$. A \emph{seed} (of geometric
type) in $\mathcal{F}$ is a pair $\Sigma = (\mathbf{x},\widetilde{B})$,
where $\mathbf{x} = (x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is a transcendence basis of
$\mathcal{F}$ and $\widetilde{B}$ is an $n\times(n+m)$ integer matrix
whose principal $n\times n$ part $B$ is skew-symmetrizable. We write
$x_{n+i} = g_i$ for $i\in[m]$, call $\widetilde{\mathbf{x}} =
(x_1,\dots,x_{n+m})$ the \emph{extended cluster}, its first $n$
entries the \emph{cluster variables}, and the remaining $m$ entries
the \emph{stable variables} (sometimes called \emph{frozen variables} or \emph{coefficients}).

Given a seed $\Sigma=(\mathbf{x},\widetilde{B})$ and $k\in[n]$, the
\emph{mutation in direction $k$} produces a new seed
$\Sigma_k=(\mathbf{x}_k,\mu_k(\widetilde{B}))$, where
$\mathbf{x}_k = (\mathbf{x}\setminus\{x_k\})\cup\{x_k'\}$, the new
cluster variable $x_k'$ is defined by the exchange relation
\[
  x_k x_k' \;=\; \prod_{\substack{1\le i\le n+m \\ b_{ki}>0}} x_i^{\,b_{ki}}
  \;+\; \prod_{\substack{1\le i\le n+m \\ b_{ki}<0}} x_i^{-b_{ki}},
\]
and $\mu_k(\widetilde B)$ is the matrix mutation
\[
  b'_{ij} =
  \begin{cases}
    -b_{ij}, & i=k \text{ or } j=k,\\[2pt]
    b_{ij} + \dfrac{|b_{ik}|b_{kj}+b_{ik}|b_{kj}|}{2}, & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
\]
Seed mutation is an involution with $\mu_k(\mu_k(\widetilde B))=\widetilde B$.
Two seeds are \emph{mutation equivalent} if related by a finite
sequence of mutations; the set of all seeds mutation equivalent to a
given one is called a \emph{cluster structure} $\mathcal{C}$ in
$\mathcal{F}$. The \emph{cluster algebra} $\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{C})$
is the subring of $\mathcal{F}$ generated by all cluster variables
occurring in all seeds of $\mathcal{C}$; the \emph{upper cluster
algebra} $\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{C})$ is the intersection,
over all seeds, of the rings of Laurent polynomials in the
corresponding extended cluster. By the Laurent phenomenon,
$\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{C})$.
A cluster structure $\mathcal{C}$ together with a field isomorphism
$\varphi:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{C}(V)$ for an algebraic variety $V$ is
called \emph{regular} if $\varphi$ sends every cluster and stable
variable, in every seed, to a regular function on $V$; when in
addition $\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbb C}(\mathcal{C})$ is
isomorphic to $\mathcal{O}(V)$ via $\varphi$, we say $\mathcal{C}$ is
a cluster structure \emph{on} $V$.

\subsection{Poisson brackets compatible with a cluster structure}

Let $\{\cdot,\cdot\}$ be a Poisson bracket on the field $\mathcal{F}$
and $\mathcal{C}$ a cluster structure in $\mathcal{F}$. Following
Gekhtman--Shapiro--Vainshtein \cite{GSV03}, we say $\{\cdot,\cdot\}$
and $\mathcal{C}$ are \emph{compatible} if, for every extended
cluster $\widetilde{\mathbf x}=(x_1,\dots,x_{n+m})$ occurring in
$\mathcal{C}$ and any pair $i,j\in 1,\ldots,n+m$ there is a constant
$\omega_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logcanonical}
  \{x_i,x_j\} = \omega_{ij}\, x_i x_j.
\end{equation}
A bracket satisfying \eqref{eq:logcanonical} in a given basis is
called \emph{log-canonical} in that basis, and the skew-symmetric
matrix $\Omega^{\widetilde{\mathbf x}} = (\omega_{ij})$ is its
\emph{coefficient matrix}. A basic result of \cite{GSV03} (see also
\cite[Ch.~4]{GSV10}) characterizes compatibility linearly: when
$\operatorname{rank}\widetilde B = n$, a Poisson bracket with
coefficient matrix $\Omega^{\widetilde{\mathbf x}}$ is compatible
with $\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(\widetilde B)$ if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:compatibility}
  \widetilde{B}\,\Omega^{\widetilde{\mathbf x}} = (D\ \ 0),
\end{equation}
for some diagonal matrix $D$. Because \eqref{eq:compatibility}
transforms correctly under mutation, compatibility need only be
verified for a single seed of $\mathcal{C}$; it then holds
automatically for every seed obtained from it by mutation. Under
compatibility, cluster mutations act as Poisson maps between the
corresponding coordinate charts (up to rescaling by the exchange
relation), so that cluster monomials furnish an explicit
log-canonical coordinate system for
$(\mathcal{F},\{\cdot,\cdot\})$.

\subsection{Belavin--Drinfeld classification and Sklyanin brackets}

Let $\mathcal G$ be a simple complex Lie group with Lie algebra
$\mathfrak g$, Cartan subalgebra $\mathfrak h$, root system $\Phi$,
positive roots $\Phi^+$ and simple roots $\Delta\subset\Phi^+$. A
\emph{classical $R$-matrix} is an element $r\in\mathfrak
g\otimes\mathfrak g$ satisfying the classical Yang--Baxter equation
$[[r,r]]=0$ together with $r+r^{21}=\mathfrak t$, where $\mathfrak
t\in\mathfrak g\otimes\mathfrak g$ is the Casimir element associated
to a fixed nondegenerate invariant bilinear form on $\mathfrak g$.
Given such an $r=\sum_{i,j} r_{ij}I_i\otimes I_j$ in a basis
$\{I_i\}$ of $\mathfrak g$, the associated \emph{Sklyanin bracket}
on $\mathcal G$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sklyanin}
  \{f_1,f_2\} = \sum_{i,j} r_{ij}\left(
  \partial_i^R f_1\, \partial_j^R f_2 - \partial_i^L f_1\, \partial_j^L f_2
  \right),
\end{equation}
where $\partial_i^{L},\partial_i^{R}$ denote the left-invariant and
right-invariant vector fields with value $I_i$ at the identity
\cite[Prop.~4.1.4]{KS98algebras}. This bracket makes $\mathcal
G$ into a \emph{Poisson--Lie group}: the multiplication map $\mathcal
G\times\mathcal G\to\mathcal G$ is Poisson.

Belavin and Drinfeld classified all solutions of the classical
Yang--Baxter equation on a simple Lie algebra \cite{BD82}. A
\emph{Belavin--Drinfeld triple} is a triple $T=(\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2,\gamma)$,
where $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2\subset\Delta$ and $\gamma:\Gamma_1\to\Gamma_2$
is an isometry that is \emph{nilpotent}, in the sense that for every
$\alpha\in\Gamma_1$ there is $m\in\mathbb N$ with $\gamma^j(\alpha)\in
\Gamma_1$ for $j<m$ but $\gamma^m(\alpha)\notin\Gamma_1$. The map
$\gamma$ extends to the root systems generated by $\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2$
and induces a partial order $\alpha\prec_T\beta$ on $\Phi$ whenever
$\beta=\gamma^j(\alpha)$ for some $j\ge 0$. Up to the action of an
automorphism $\sigma\otimes\sigma$ of $\mathfrak g\otimes\mathfrak g$,
every classical $R$-matrix has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BDform}
  r \;=\; r_0 \;+\; \sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^+} e_{-\alpha}\otimes e_\alpha
  \;+\; \sum_{\substack{\alpha\prec_T\beta\\ \alpha,\beta\in\Phi^+}}
  e_{-\alpha}\wedge e_\beta,
\end{equation}
where $r_0\in\mathfrak h\otimes\mathfrak h$ satisfies a linear system
determined by $T$; the space of such $r_0$ has dimension
$k_T(k_T-1)/2$, where $k_T=|\Delta\setminus\Gamma_1|$
\cite[Prop.~3.1]{GSV12cluster}, following the classification in
\cite{BD82}. More details can be found in~\cite{CP95guide}.

Two $R$-matrices of the form \eqref{eq:BDform} belong to
the same \emph{Belavin--Drinfeld class} if they share the same
triple $T$. The \emph{trivial} triple, $\Gamma_1=\Gamma_2=\emptyset$,
gives $k_T = |\Delta| = \operatorname{rank}\mathcal G$ and recovers
the \emph{standard} Sklyanin bracket, unique up to the choice of
$r_0\in\mathfrak h\wedge\mathfrak h$. Any triple with
$\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2\neq\emptyset$ is called \emph{non-trivial}, and
the corresponding brackets \emph{non-standard}. The
\emph{Cremmer--Gervais} triple, with $\Gamma_1=\{\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_{n-2}\}$,
$\Gamma_2=\{\alpha_2,\dots,\alpha_{n-1}\}$, $\gamma(\alpha_i)=\alpha_{i+1}$
in type $A_{n-1}$, is the triple of maximal size with $k_T=1$.

Following~\cite{GSV24plethora} we generalize the definition of the bracket: we use two BD triples to get two classical $R$ matrices $r^L, r^R$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:genPsnBrckt}
  \{f_1,f_2\} = \sum_{i,j} r^R_{ij}
  \partial_i^R f_1\, \partial_j^R f_2 -
  r^L_{i,j}\partial_i^L f_1\, \partial_j^L f_2.
\end{equation}
Note that this defines a Poisson bracket that is not necessarily Poisson--Lie (unless $r^L = r^R$).

This paper will address only \emph{aperiodic} Belavin--Drinfeld triples as defined in~\cite{GSV24plethora}: we define the BD graph as a graph with two copies of the simple roots as numbered vertices $1,\ldots ,n$ in two rows, one above the other. In each row, vertices $i$ and $n+1-i$ are connected by an edge. In addition, for every pair $(\alpha_i,\gamma _r(\alpha_i))$ there is an edge connecting $i$ on the top row to $\gamma _r(i)$ on the bottom row (slightly abusing the notation we write $j=\gamma _r(i)$ wherever $\alpha _j = \gamma _r(\alpha _i)$). A connected component of the BD graph can be a simple path, a cycle, a path with a loop at one end or a path with a loop at both ends. A BD triple is called \emph{aperiodic} every connected component of the corresponding BD graph is either a paths or a path with one loop. Otherwise we say that the BD triple is \emph{periodic}.

\section{Combinatorial model for generalized cluster structures on $SL_{n+1}$}
We begin by recalling the construction of \newword{double wiring diagrams} from~\cite{FZ99}.
Let $W$ be the Weyl group of $SL_{n+1}$; it is the symmetric group on $n+1$ letters but we regard it as the Coxeter group generated by $n$ simple reflections $s_i$ subject to the braid relations $s_is_{i+1}s_i=s_{i+1}s_is_{i+1}$.
Let $w_0$ be the longest element in $W$.
A word $(i_1,\dots,i_\ell)$ in the alphabet $[1,n]$ is a reduced word for $w\in W$ if $s_{i_1}\dots s_{i_\ell}$ is a reduced expression of $w$.
Given a pair of elements $u,v$ in $W$ a \newword{double reduced word} for $(u,v)$ is an arbitrary shuffle $\bi$ of two reduced words, one for $u$ in the alphabeth $[-1,-n]$ and one for $v$ in the alphabeth $[1,n]$. 

Given a double deduced word $\bi$, the \newword{double wiring diagram} is a graphical encoding of $\bi$ built as follows.
There are $n+1$ horizontal lines, they are numbered from the bottom up.
To each entry $i$ of $\bi$ it corresponds a bridge connecting line $|i|$ to line $|i+1|$. 
Bridges are of two types, positive and negative, corresponding to the sign of $i$.
Positive bridges are depicted with a bullet on the bottom endpoint and a cirle on the top endpoint.
For negative bridges the endpoint markings are swapped.

As a running example pick $n=4$ and consider the double reduced word 
\[
  \bi=(-1, 2, 1, -2, 2, -3, 3, -4, 4, 3, 2, -3, 3, -2, 1, 2, -1, -2, -3, -2)
\]

To each double wiring diagram, hence to each double reduced word, one can associate a quiver as follows.
Draw one vertex in each of the cells of the diagram. 
Vertices in unbounded cells are frozen and depicted with a square.
Vertices in bounded cells are mutable and depicted with a bullet.
For each segment in the diagram, either horizontal or vertical, having differently marked endpoints draw one arrow in between the two nodes corresponding to the two cells separated by the segment.
The arrow is directed as to leave the bullet on the right.

Double reduced words define also a collection of minors as follows.
For any $i\in[1,n+1]$ the $i$-th negative strand starts on the $i$-th horizontal line on the left of the picture and moves rightwards crossing any negative bridge it encounters.
Similarly the $i$-th positive strand starts on the $i$-th horizontal line on the right and moves leftwards crossing any positive bridge it encounters.
To any chamber associate then the minor whose rows (resp. columns) are indexed by the positive (res. negative) strands passing below that chamber.

By the construction in~\cite{BFZ05} adapted to our language says that the quiver and the minors associated to any double reduced word for $(w_0,w_0)$ give a seed in the standard cluster structure on $SL_{n+1}$.

Let $\gamma_r:\Gamma_r\subset\Gamma\rightarrow\Gamma$ and $\gamma_c:\Gamma_c\subset\Gamma\rightarrow\Gamma$ be a pair of aperiodic BD triples for $SL_{n+1}$.
To simplify the construction our conventions deviate from those in~\cite{GSV23}. 
Namely we are denoting by $\gamma_c$ what is there denoted by $\gamma_c^*$.

For any double reduced word $\bi$ for $(w_0,w_0)$ we are going to construct a seed in the cluster structure on $SL_{n+1}$ defined by the pair $\gamma_r,\gamma_c$ by modifying the standard seed associated to $\bi$.

Begin by drawing the double wiring diagram for $\bi$ and its dual quiver.
Choose $|\Gamma_r|+|\Gamma_c|$ different colors and assign them to the elements of the domains of $\gamma_r$ and $\gamma_c$.

For each $i\in\Gamma_r$ do the following: 
\begin{itemize}
  \item 
    Trace the $\gamma_r(i)$-th and $(\gamma_r(i)+1)$-th positive strands from the right leftwards until they cross the same bridge. 
    Call this bridge the \newword{$i$-th positive inversion bridge}.
    Shade the cells enclosed by the strands just traced with the color associated to $i$.

  \item 
    Unfreze the $i_*$-th (i.e. the $(n+1-i)$-th) node on the left and color it with the color associated to $i$.
    Call this node the \newword{$i$-th tail node}.

  \item
    Add a ``virtual'' positive bridge to the left of the $i$-th tail node. 
    If $k\in\Gamma_r$ is such that $|i-k|=1$ then the relative order of the virtual bridges to be added for $i$ and $k$ is the opposite of the order of the corresponding inversion bridges.

  \item
    Use virtual bridges to add arrows to the quiver following the same rules as for the other arrows.

  \item
    Add one arrow from the node immediately right of the $i$-th positive inversion bridge to the $i$-th tail node.
    Add one arrow from the $i$-th tail node to the node immediately left of the $i$-th positive inversion bridge.
\end{itemize}

For each $i\in\Gamma_c$ follow the same rules as above replacing everywhere negative for positive and left for right.

To define the new functions associated to the vertices of the quiver multiply the function in any shaded cell by the function at the corresponding tail node. 
If a cell is shaded by several colors the function gets multiplied more than once.
The functions associated to tail nodes may in turn be multiplied by some other function; the aperiodicity of the pair $(\gamma_r,\gamma_c)$ guarantees that the process is well defined.

Finally apply $h^{-1}_*$ to all the functions.
Observe that functions that correspond to cells not shaded will be unchanged by $h^{-1}_*$.
